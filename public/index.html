01 / The research question

How can a collaborator
anticipate the next action?

Assembly depends on more than recognising a movement. A partner needs to understand which part is needed, whether it will be passed or placed, and when to act.

QUB-PHEO provides observations for studying that problem. A reaching hand, the object being handled, a change in gaze and the preceding task sequence can each provide evidence about the next step. Researchers can examine those cues together, rather than infer intent from an isolated image.

The contribution is a dataset of dyadic assembly interactions, multiple camera perspectives and fine-grained subtask labels. It supports the development and evaluation of perception and anticipation methods; the recordings themselves do not establish that a robot can infer intent reliably.

Study design and original contribution: Adebayo, McLoone & Dessing, IEEE Access (2024).

02 / Experimental design

Two people.
A surrogate robot role.

A participant and an attending researcher worked opposite one another at a table. The researcher acted as the robot surrogate, following a structured interaction protocol.

This choice allowed collaborative behaviour to be studied without the capabilities of a particular robot determining the interaction. The surrogate was a human collaborator, rather than a physical robot or an attempt to reproduce robot motion.

Five GoPro cameras recorded the workspace: an overhead view for the assembly and hand–object interactions, and four side views for complementary observations of the participant and workbench. DUPLO blocks, a green-screen background and controlled lighting provided a repeatable laboratory setting.

The study recruited 70 participants and received ethics approval from Queen’s University Belfast’s Faculty of Engineering and Physical Sciences. Distribution follows each participant’s release consent; inclusion in the study does not imply that their video may be shared.

03 / Tasks and temporal labels

Four assembly families.
Nine task variations.

Task families describe the assembly goal. Subtask labels describe the smaller actions within it.

01 · THREE VARIATIONS

Block in a Hole

Aligning and fitting a block into a target arrangement. The interaction makes part selection and precise placement observable.

02 · TWO VARIATIONS

Simple Tower

Constructing a layered structure through coordinated selection, transfer and stacking of blocks.

03 · TWO VARIATIONS

Stairway Shuffle

Building through a sequence of placements, with variations in pointing and collaborative stacking.

04 · TWO VARIATIONS

Bridge Building

Combining supports and spanning pieces, with task variants that change how the partners coordinate.

Why subtask boundaries matter

The published annotation scheme has 36 subtask classes. A whole assembly trial contains a sequence of shorter actions, so a learner can study the current action, its transition and the next action. The sample selector preserves the original task variant and subtask codes; these are labels, not prediction scores.

A model evaluated on complete labelled clips answers a recognition question. Anticipating what happens next requires limiting its input to the observations available before that future action. This distinction matters when defining an experiment.

04 / From recordings to visual cues

Record. Annotate.
Extract. Inspect.

  1. Record and calibrate

    The published acquisition pipeline used five GoPro Hero10 cameras, calibration and synchronisation procedures to capture the interaction from several perspectives.

  2. Annotate the action sequence

    Temporal labels identify the assembly subtasks. Annotation and segmentation associate those labels with the relevant video intervals and camera views.

  3. Extract visual signals

    Video processing produces facial, body, hand, gaze and object cues. These are model-derived estimates, distinct from the task annotations. The current viewer uses the saved landmark files supplied with the sample clips.

  4. Inspect the browser examples

    The public viewer pairs each clip with its matching saved predictions. Silent, smaller H.264 previews preserve the source frames and relative timing. Coordinates remain in the original image system; no new inference runs when you press Play.

06 / Research uses and evaluation

Ask a precise
prediction question.

Recognise the current subtask

Estimate the action label from a defined video or landmark observation window. Report whether the complete action or only its early frames were available.

Anticipate the next step

Predict a future subtask from past observations and task history. State the observation window and prediction horizon so future frames cannot leak into the input.

Compare views and cues

Measure what hands, gaze, body movement and object context add individually and together. Compare single-view and multi-view methods under the same split and observation budget.

Model dyadic visual motion

Study how the two collaborators’ movements evolve over time. The published motion-prediction baseline resources provide a separate starting point.

Suggested evaluation practice. Use participant-disjoint splits when testing generalisation to unseen people. Keep related views and overlapping clips together within a split; report task/class balance and missing-cue coverage. The 20 browser examples are a coverage-based demonstration, not a benchmark or an unbiased sample.

07 / Release scope and limitations

What this dataset
can—and cannot—show.

ScopeCoverageAccess

Consented video release50 participantsFull dataset on Drive, subject to the official EULA and Seán’s approval.
Public explorer20 examples from 20 participants and 20 subtasks; all five camerasPublic playback of these selected examples and matching saved landmarks.

Human–human observations, laboratory setting

The surrogate design supports research relevant to HRI, but transfer to a physical robot, factory conditions or a new population needs its own evaluation.

Extracted cues are not motion-capture ground truth

Occlusion and model errors affect landmarks, gaze and detections. The displayed coordinates are 2D image measurements; this viewer does not turn them into a calibrated 3D reconstruction or certify their accuracy.

Timing and release versions matter

Playback follows existing relative clip timestamps. Lower-view legacy landmarks use video-frame row timing. The previews do not independently estimate new physical camera offsets, and their frame rates should not be inferred from the original acquisition settings.
